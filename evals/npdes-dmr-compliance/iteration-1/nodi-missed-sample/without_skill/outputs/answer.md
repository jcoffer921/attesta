Short answer: report the one sample you have, use a NODI code only if there is no value at all for a parameter/period, and yes, a missed required sample is a monitoring/reporting violation even though the one result may be under the limit.

## What to enter on the eDMR

- Enter the one valid TSS result you collected. Do not put a NODI code on the whole TSS line and do not leave the value blank.
- With one sample, the monthly average equals that single result, and the daily maximum is the same value. Report them from the sample you have. Do not average in a zero or estimate a value for the missed sample.
- Under the "Number of Excursions"/"No. of Samples" style fields (PA eDMR asks for sample frequency fields on some parameters), enter the actual number of samples taken (1) if your report has such a field, versus the required 2.
- NODI codes apply when no data exist for a reportable value (for example, no discharge, or the whole parameter not sampled). NODI codes commonly used: C (no discharge), 9 (conditional monitoring not required this period), B (below detection limit, only for results below the QL, and generally not applicable here), and others such as 7 or similar for "not required." None of them describes "sample missed because the equipment failed", so do not use a NODI code to cover the missed second sample. Confirm the exact code list in the PA DEP eDMR instructions.
- Use the comments/attachment field on the eDMR to explain: the autosampler failed in the first half of September, the date it failed, what you did (repair or replace date), and that only one sample was collected instead of two.

## Is it a violation?

Yes, probably. Collecting fewer samples than the permit's minimum frequency is a violation of the monitoring requirements in the permit, regardless of the result. Under 40 CFR 122.41(j) and (l) and the PA permit's Part A monitoring conditions, you must monitor at the frequency specified. It is usually a monitoring/reporting violation, not an effluent limit exceedance. That distinction matters:

- If the single result is below the TSS monthly average and daily max limits, there is no effluent exceedance.
- The missed sample is still a monitoring frequency violation that DEP can cite, and it will show up in compliance tracking. It is generally a lower-severity violation than an exceedance, but repeated missed samples can raise enforcement concern.

## What to do

1. Take a grab sample as soon as possible if the permit allows grab samples as a substitute. Check whether your permit allows a grab sample when the autosampler is out of service. Some permits do, and if collected in the same month, it may not fix the month already ended.
2. Report the missed sample accurately on the DMR and explain in the comments. Do not report a made-up value.
3. Notify the DEP regional office (clean water program) in writing about the equipment failure and missed sampling, and ask how they want it handled. Some permits require noncompliance reporting under the "Reporting noncompliance" conditions (Part A) within 5 days for other noncompliance, and 24 hours for certain events. A missed sample is generally reported on the DMR, and DEP may want the written explanation.
4. Document the repair and put a backup plan in place (spare sampler, rental unit, or an approved grab-sample procedure).

## Caveats

I cannot see your permit, so check the exact monitoring frequency language (for example "2/month" versus "1/week"), whether it allows alternate sampling, and the current eDMR code list in the PA DEP eDMR user guide. Treat this as general guidance and confirm with your DEP regional compliance specialist, since they decide how the missed sample is handled.
